Undergraduate Research Assistant

01 Introduction

Computing in a
student support setting.

During my Summer 2026 co-op term, I worked as an Undergraduate Research Assistant in AI in Academic Skill Development at McLaughlin Library, University of Guelph.

The term showed me how software, data, and AI can support learning when they are built carefully. Much of my work focused on making information easier to organize, helping staff compare tools more clearly, and creating workflows that could be reviewed, maintained, and used beyond one person’s desk.

02 About the employer

The role of
McLaughlin Library.

McLaughlin Library is a central part of academic life at the University of Guelph. It supports students, staff, and faculty through study, writing, data, research, publishing, teaching, and accessibility services, including workshops and one-on-one appointments. It is also home to learning supports that help students build skills in areas such as research, technology, writing, numeracy, studying, and time management.

My work connected to that mission by focusing on how technology can make academic support more usable, inclusive, and sustainable. As AI becomes more visible in learning environments, libraries have an important role in helping people ask better questions: what a tool does well, what risks it creates, how accessible it is, and how students and staff can use it responsibly.

03 My role

One role,
four work streams.

My role was less about one fixed task and more about supporting the library’s digital and academic-skills work from several angles. I moved between data preparation, AI-supported workflows, Microsoft 365 tools, and research tasks, always focusing on work that could be checked, explained, and handed off clearly.

01

Automation & data

I helped turn messy or manual information into structured, reviewable data. That included collecting information, cleaning records, checking for inconsistencies, and preparing outputs that staff could inspect instead of treating automation as a black box.

Work examples

Built repeatable scripts, cleaned spreadsheet-based records, organized extracted information, and added review steps for cases that needed human judgment.

Why it helped

The work made information easier to compare, update, and reuse, while keeping staff in control of final decisions.

  • Python
  • Requests
  • BeautifulSoup
  • OpenPyXL
  • NumPy
  • Seaborn

02

Prompt engineering

I used prompt design as a practical workflow skill, not just an AI shortcut. I tested how prompts changed the consistency, usefulness, and limits of outputs, then adjusted instructions so results were easier to review.

Work examples

Drafted and refined prompts for classification, summaries, and structured outputs, then compared results against expected formats and documented what still needed review.

Why it helped

It helped make AI-supported work more transparent, repeatable, and careful about accuracy instead of treating outputs as automatically correct.

  • Prompt design
  • Output review
  • Documentation
  • Quality checks

03

Microsoft 365 development

I worked with Microsoft 365 as a way to connect familiar staff tools into clearer workflows. The goal was to reduce repeated manual steps and make routine processes easier to track.

Work examples

Prototyped connected forms, lists, notifications, workbook logic, and staff-facing views, then tested whether the flow matched how people would actually use it.

Why it helped

These tools supported clearer handoffs, fewer hidden steps, and more visible status tracking for staff workflows.

  • Forms
  • SharePoint
  • Power Automate
  • Teams
  • Power Apps
  • Excel

04

Research & evaluation

I also supported research into AI and assistive technologies for academic skill development. This meant looking beyond features and asking how tools might affect access, privacy, learning, and implementation.

Work examples

Compared tools using shared criteria, reviewed guidance and policy themes, prepared research materials, and helped gather feedback connected to student learning needs.

Why it helped

The work gave staff a more balanced way to think about AI: not just what tools can do, but who they support, what risks they introduce, and where human guidance matters.

  • Canva
  • Clipchamp
  • Qualtrics
  • Evaluation criteria
04 Goals & learning outcomes

Goals
for the term.

My main goal was to grow from completing assigned tasks into building thoughtful, reusable work. I wanted to become stronger at solving messy problems, communicating technical choices, and thinking about how data and AI tools affect real learning environments.

01

Build repeatable workflows

I wanted to practise turning manual work into processes that could be checked, reused, and explained after I handed them off.

02

Connect tools to staff needs

I wanted to understand what people actually needed from a tool before deciding what to build or automate.

03

Evaluate AI responsibly

I wanted to look at AI and assistive technologies through usefulness, accessibility, privacy, and student support, not just through features.

Skills gained

The term helped me build both technical confidence and the professional habits that make technical work useful to a team.

Technical

  • Python automation
  • Data cleaning and validation
  • Spreadsheet logic
  • Microsoft 365 workflows
  • Prompt engineering
  • Research synthesis

Professional

  • Asking clearer questions
  • Testing with feedback
  • Writing documentation
  • Explaining technical choices
  • Working independently
  • Thinking about maintainability

Looking ahead

These skills connect directly to my future goal of becoming a data scientist. Data science is not only about models or code; it also depends on understanding messy information, checking assumptions, explaining results, and building workflows that other people can trust.

05 Conclusion

What I am
taking forward.

The biggest lesson from this term was that technical work is strongest when it is clear enough for other people to trust.

Across data workflows, Microsoft 365 tools, prompt engineering, and AI research, I learned that building something is only part of the job. The work also needs validation, documentation, feedback, and a careful understanding of who will use it.

I am grateful to the staff at McLaughlin Library for welcoming me into this work, and especially to Melanie Parlette-Stewart for her supervision, guidance, and support throughout the term.
